Six Document Tools That Don't Mess Around

Convert PDFs, compress bloated files, merge and split documents, lock them down, and sign them off — all inside your browser, all free, and nothing ever touches a server.

✦ 100% Browser-Based ✦ No Server Uploads ✦ 6 Document Tools ✦ Updated April 2025

Here is a number that stuck with me longer than it should have: the average office worker spends somewhere around 2.5 hours every single day looking for documents. That comes from a McKinsey study from 2023, and when I first read it, I assumed it was one of those inflated statistics that consultancies love to throw around. Then I actually timed myself for a week. Turns out the number might even be low.

The problem is not just finding documents. It is converting them, compressing them, combining them, protecting them, and getting them signed. Each of those tasks sounds simple in theory, but in practice it means opening some clunky desktop application, waiting for it to load, wrestling with an interface that has not been updated since Windows 7, and then — if you are using an online tool — crossing your fingers that the service does not store a copy of your confidential files on some server you have never heard of.

We got tired of that cycle. So we built six document tools that handle the most common PDF and document tasks people run into every week. I am going to walk through each one honestly — what it does well, where it struggles, and who it is actually built for. No marketing fluff. These tools are not trying to replace Adobe Acrobat or Microsoft Word. They are built for the other 90% of document tasks that should not require a $240/year subscription.

PDF to Word (and Back): The Conversion Tool You Actually Need

Tool #1

Bidirectional PDF and Word Conversion

This is probably the tool that gets the most daily use across all six, and for good reason. PDF-to-Word conversion is one of those problems that sounds trivial until you actually try to do it well. Most free converters scramble the layout, mangle tables, drop images entirely, or convert everything into plain text that looks like it was typed on a typewriter from 1987. Our converter does not do that — at least not usually.

The way it works is straightforward. You upload a PDF, the tool parses the document structure — paragraphs, headings, tables, images, font styles — and reconstructs it as an editable DOCX file. The PDF to Word direction preserves the layout with maybe 85–95% accuracy depending on how the original PDF was created. If the PDF was generated from Word in the first place (which most business documents are), the reconstruction is nearly perfect. If it is a scanned image PDF, well — that is a different story, and I will be honest about that in a moment.

The Word to PDF direction is simpler and more reliable. You upload a DOCX file, and the tool generates a pixel-perfect PDF that looks exactly the same on every device, every operating system, every PDF reader. This is genuinely useful when you need to send a document to someone and you do not want them accidentally editing it, or when a platform requires PDF submissions specifically (government forms, journal submissions, grant applications — that kind of thing).

What people use this for most:

  • Editing locked PDFs — Someone sends you a PDF that you need to modify. Instead of retyping the whole thing, convert it to Word, make your edits, and convert it back. Saves maybe 20–40 minutes per document depending on length
  • Resume updates — Your resume is a PDF (it should be), but you need to update it. Convert to Word, tweak the content, export back to PDF. Clean and fast
  • Academic submissions — Professors often want submissions in Word format for grading, but you have a PDF version. The conversion preserves the formatting well enough that most professors would not notice
  • Legal document editing — Contracts, agreements, and formal letters often arrive as PDFs. Converting them to Word makes redlining and revision-tracking actually possible

One thing I want to be upfront about: if your PDF is a scanned image — like a photo of a paper document taken with your phone — the conversion will not work well. There is no OCR (optical character recognition) built in, so the tool cannot read text from images. For scanned documents, you need a dedicated OCR tool first. That is a limitation we may address in the future, but I do not want to pretend the current version handles it when it does not.

PDF Compressor: Shrink Bloated Files Without Destroying Them

Tool #2

Intelligent PDF Size Reduction

PDFs have this annoying habit of becoming enormous. A 15-page report with a few embedded images can easily balloon to 25, 30, even 50 MB — which is fine on your local machine but becomes a real headache when you need to email it, upload it to a portal with a 10 MB limit, or send it through a messaging app. I have personally had emails bounce back because a PDF attachment exceeded the recipient's server limit. It is frustrating and, honestly, kind of embarrassing in a professional context.

Our PDF compressor attacks the problem from three angles. First, it optimizes embedded images — resizing where possible, converting to more efficient formats, and stripping unnecessary metadata. Second, it compresses and subsets fonts, so if your document uses only 40% of the characters in a font file, the other 60% gets dropped. Third, it removes redundancies in the PDF structure itself — duplicate objects, unused data streams, that sort of thing.

In our testing across roughly 200 documents of varying types, the average compression ratio lands somewhere between 50% and 70% size reduction. For documents that are mostly text with a few images, you can hit 80%+. For image-heavy documents like product catalogs or design portfolios, the reduction is more modest — maybe 30–50% — because there is only so much you can compress an image before it starts looking bad.

The tool offers multiple compression levels so you are not stuck with a one-size-fits-all approach. The "recommended" setting strikes a balance between file size and visual quality that works for most business documents. If you need maximum compression (say, for a quick email attachment where perfect image clarity is not critical), crank it up. If the document is going to print and needs to look sharp, use the lighter setting.

Real scenarios where this saves the day:

  • Email attachments — Most email servers cap attachments at 20–25 MB. A compressed PDF slides right through without any issues
  • Form uploads — Government portals, job application systems, and university submission platforms often have strict file size limits. Compress first, upload without stress
  • Cloud storage — If you are paying for storage by the gigabyte (Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud), compressing your PDF archive can free up meaningful space
  • Faster sharing via messaging — WhatsApp, Telegram, and Slack all struggle with large files. A 40 MB PDF compressed to 8 MB sends instantly
The tool processes everything in your browser. Your documents never leave your device. I know every tool claims this, but in this case it is actually verifiable — open your browser's network tab while compressing a file and you will see zero outbound requests.

PDF Merger and Splitter: Combine and Divide Documents in Seconds

Tool #3

Document Merging and Page Extraction

These are technically two separate functions, but they solve the same basic problem: PDFs are annoying to reorganize. You cannot just drag pages around in a standard PDF viewer. You cannot easily pull one chapter out of a 200-page report. And if someone sends you seven separate PDFs that need to be combined into a single package, your options are basically "install software" or "use a sketchy online service that might watermark your output."

The merger lets you upload any number of PDFs and combine them into one. Drag to reorder, click merge, done. There is no file count limit — we have tested it with 30+ files in a single merge operation. The output is a single PDF that maintains the original quality and formatting of every source document. Page numbers are re-indexed automatically, and bookmarks from the original files are preserved.

The splitter does the opposite: it lets you break a PDF into smaller pieces. You can extract specific page ranges (pages 1–12, or pages 45–60), pull individual pages, or split every page into its own separate file. The last option sounds niche, but it comes up more than you would think — extracting individual invoice pages from a monthly statement, separating pages from a scanned textbook for study, or pulling out specific forms from a multi-page government document.

The workflows where this matters most:

  • Proposal and report compilation — Sales teams combine a cover letter, company overview, case studies, pricing table, and terms into one polished PDF. Before this tool, that meant printing everything and scanning it back as a single document. Yes, people actually still do that
  • Academic paper submissions — Most journals want a single PDF with your manuscript, figures, and supplementary materials combined. The merger handles this without quality loss
  • Legal document management — Lawyers regularly need to compile exhibits, appendices, and supporting documents into a single court filing. The ability to reorder and merge on the fly is genuinely time-saving
  • Invoice and receipt processing — Extract individual pages from a multi-page PDF statement, or combine multiple receipts into a single expense report document

Processing speed depends on your device. On a modern laptop, merging ten 50-page PDFs takes maybe 5–10 seconds. Splitting is even faster since it is essentially just extracting page ranges. On older devices or very large files (100+ pages), expect to wait a bit longer — but it is still faster than opening Adobe Acrobat and waiting for it to load.

Document Converter: Handle Every Format Without Installing Anything

Tool #4

Multi-Format Document Conversion

Not everything is a PDF, and not everyone uses Word. In the real world, documents circulate in DOCX, PDF, TXT, RTF, and ODT formats — sometimes all within the same organization. The marketing team works in Google Docs (which exports to DOCX). The legal team insists on PDF. The IT department sends documentation in TXT. Someone's old system still generates RTF files. And the open-source people in the corner use ODT because, well, principles.

The Document Converter handles all of these formats and converts between any combination. The core supported formats are DOCX, PDF, TXT, RTF, and ODT. You pick your source file, choose the output format, and the tool handles the rest. It preserves tables, images, font styling, headers and footers, and page structure. The conversion is not always pixel-perfect — going from a heavily formatted DOCX to plain TXT naturally strips all formatting — but for the format combinations that matter (DOCX to PDF, PDF to DOCX, RTF to DOCX), the output quality is solid.

I personally use this tool most often for two things: converting RTF files (which some older systems still generate) to DOCX so I can actually edit them in Google Docs, and converting DOCX proposals to PDF when a client specifically requests PDF format. Neither of these tasks is complicated, but before having a browser-based converter, I was opening LibreOffice for the RTF conversions and saving-as-PDF in Word for the other direction. This tool collapses both workflows into about ten seconds.

Where this tool fits into real workflows:

  • Cross-platform compatibility — Someone sends you an ODT file and you do not have OpenOffice installed. Convert it to DOCX and open it in Word or Google Docs
  • Archive and compliance — Convert active DOCX documents to PDF for long-term archival. PDF is more stable for long-term storage because the formatting does not shift between software versions
  • Content extraction — Convert a PDF to TXT to pull out raw text for analysis, translation, or repurposing. Strips formatting but keeps the content
  • Legacy system integration — If you deal with older software that only accepts RTF or plain TXT input, this converter bridges the gap between modern formats and legacy requirements

PDF Lock and Unlock: Take Control of Who Sees Your Documents

Tool #5

Password Protection and Document Security

Document security is one of those things that nobody thinks about until they need it — and when they need it, they need it badly. I am talking about the moment you realize you just emailed an unencrypted PDF containing someone's salary information, medical records, or a confidential business strategy to the wrong distribution list. That pit-in-your-stomach feeling is not something you want to experience twice.

The Lock tool adds password protection to any PDF with configurable permission levels. You set a password, and you choose what the recipient can do with the document. Options include: allow viewing only (no printing, no copying, no editing), allow printing but not editing, or allow editing but not copying. The encryption is standard AES-256, which is the same level of encryption used by banks and government agencies. Not that your quarterly report needs bank-grade encryption, but it is nice to know it is there.

The Unlock tool does the reverse — it removes password protection from PDFs when you have the correct password. This sounds niche until you realize how many people inherit password-protected documents from former colleagues, old projects, or archived systems and no longer have the password. Or when you password-protect something for a specific purpose and then need to edit it later. The unlock process is instant: enter the password, and the tool removes the restrictions, giving you a clean, unrestricted PDF.

Who actually needs this:

  • HR departments — Salary sheets, performance reviews, and employee records should absolutely be password-protected before being stored or shared. The permission controls prevent unauthorized redistribution
  • Legal professionals — Client contracts, NDA agreements, and settlement documents often require access restrictions. The lock tool lets you control exactly what the recipient can do with the file
  • Freelancers and consultants — Sending a proposal as a locked PDF means the client can view it but cannot easily copy-paste your content or pass it off as their own
  • Healthcare — Patient records, referral letters, and insurance documentation contain sensitive information that should be protected during transit, even within a secure email system

The entire encryption and decryption process runs in your browser. The password you set never travels to a server. When you unlock a file, the decryption happens on your device using your browser's built-in crypto capabilities. This is important for compliance-heavy industries (healthcare, finance, legal) where sending unencrypted documents to third-party servers is not just risky — it might actually violate data protection regulations like HIPAA or GDPR.

eSign PDF: Sign Documents Without Printing, Scanning, or Mailing

Tool #6

Digital Document Signing

If there is one workflow that the pandemic accelerated and then never slowed down, it is electronic document signing. Before 2020, a lot of people still printed contracts, signed them with a pen, scanned the signed copy, and emailed the scan back. That entire process takes maybe 15–20 minutes if everything goes smoothly, and easily an hour if your printer decides to jam or your scanner app crashes. It is absurd when you think about it.

Our eSign tool lets you add a signature directly to a PDF in your browser. You can type your name and choose from several professional font styles, draw your signature using your mouse or touchscreen, or upload an image of your existing signature. The tool places the signature exactly where you want it on the document — drag to position, resize to fit, and you are done. The signed PDF is ready to download and send.

Now, an important question people always ask: are these signatures legally valid? The short answer is yes, in most jurisdictions. In the United States, the ESIGN Act (2000) and the UETA (Uniform Electronic Transactions Act) establish that electronic signatures carry the same legal weight as handwritten ones. In the European Union, the eIDAS regulation provides a similar framework. Our tool creates standard digital signatures that satisfy the requirements of these regulations for most common use cases: contracts, agreements, NDAs, authorization forms, and similar documents.

That said — and I think this matters — if you are dealing with something that requires a notarized signature, a court filing in a jurisdiction that specifically requires wet ink, or a real estate transaction where local laws mandate physical signing, this tool is not a substitute for those requirements. I am not giving legal advice here. I am saying that for the vast majority of everyday business agreements and personal documents, electronic signatures work fine and are recognized as valid.

How people actually use this tool:

  • Remote contract signing — Freelancers and agencies sign client agreements without ever meeting in person. The typical flow: receive contract PDF, sign it in the browser, email it back. Total time: about two minutes
  • Real estate and rental agreements — Tenants and landlords sign lease renewals electronically. Some property management companies now prefer e-signatures because they create a cleaner audit trail
  • Internal approvals — Purchase orders, budget approvals, and project authorization forms get signed and routed entirely digitally. No printing, no physical routing, no lost paperwork
  • NDA and confidentiality agreements — Startups and consulting firms send NDAs to new partners and clients. The recipient signs and returns the document within minutes instead of days
One detail people appreciate: the tool does not add a watermark, a "signed using Tool Xeno" stamp, or any branding to your signed document. The signature appears exactly as you placed it — clean, professional, and indistinguishable from a signature added with paid software.

Why Browser-Based Processing Is a Big Deal for Documents

Documents are different from audio files or images in one critical way: they often contain information that is legally and ethically sensitive. A podcast recording of you talking about your weekend plans is one thing. A PDF containing someone's tax returns, medical diagnosis, or salary history is another thing entirely.

When you upload a document to a typical online PDF tool, that document travels across the internet to a remote server, gets processed there, and the result is sent back. During that round trip, you are trusting the tool provider with your data. You are trusting their security practices, their employee policies, their data retention rules, and their judgment. Most people never think about this because the alternative — installing desktop software — is cumbersome enough that they just accept the risk.

Browser-based processing eliminates that trust equation entirely. Your documents do not leave your device. The JavaScript running in your browser tab does the work that would normally happen on a remote server. No upload, no download of your original file to a third party, no data retention, no server logs containing your document contents. It is the difference between handing a photocopy of your financial statements to a stranger and keeping the originals locked in your filing cabinet.

The performance tradeoff is real. A server with dedicated hardware is always going to process a 200 MB PDF faster than a browser running on a laptop with 8 GB of RAM. I am not going to pretend otherwise. But for the vast majority of everyday documents — contracts, reports, resumes, invoices, proposals — the processing time difference is measured in seconds, not minutes. And the privacy benefit is not measured in seconds at all.
